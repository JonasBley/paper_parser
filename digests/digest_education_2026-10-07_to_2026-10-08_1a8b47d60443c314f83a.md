<summary>Abstract</summary>

Interactive quantum circuit simulators are increasingly used to support introductory quantum computing education for learners from varied disciplinary backgrounds. These environments can also generate detailed response logs of submitted circuits, but such data are rarely transformed into evidence that instructors can use for course redesign and development. We present a learning analytics framework for quantum circuit simulator tasks and report its task-level branch on anonymised logs from an introductory quantum computing course delivered in the TIM learning environment. The task branch characterises exercises through participation, completion, first-attempt accuracy, valid-attempt counts, sessionised active time, and observed answer-space reporting of common correct and incorrect constructions. The dataset covers the 2024-2025 cohort of 492 learners, 47,533 valid submissions, and 21 obligatory quantum circuit exercises. The framework identifies bottleneck tasks, broad solution spaces, and recurring wrong-answer patterns that are not visible from final scores alone. Its most distinctive output is a plain-text representation of the observed answer space, designed to make open-ended circuit submissions directly interpretable to instructors. We show how response logs can reveal actionable task-design issues and support concrete course redesign. A learner-level branch is retained only as an ongoing research extension and is not a central empirical contribution of the present paper.

<summary>Abstract</summary>

ABSTRACT Opening space for students to help shape knowledge building practices in the classroom—that is, epistemic agency—is necessary to achieve a variety of goals held by the science education community. As such, many scholars have explored how students might be positioned with, perceive, and take up epistemic agency. Nonetheless, the field remains unclear and vague about what, exactly, “epistemic agency” means. This is a problem. Without a more precise way to think and talk about epistemic agency, we cannot communicate clearly about agentic moments, design for agentic moments, or consider whether and how these moments build toward our larger goals. To address this problem, we propose a model for parsing epistemic agency: (1) the substance of students' epistemic decision‐making, consisting of three facets, the epistemic Aims, epistemic Ideals, and Reliable processes for pursuing those aims (Chinn et al. 2014); and (2) the consequentiality of students' epistemic decision‐making, characterized in terms of two dimensions— spatial and temporal reach of students' epistemic decisions. We then demonstrate the utility of this multifaceted, multidimensional conceptualization of epistemic agency by comparing two published accounts of students making decisions about useful ways to know and learn. Attending to the multifaceted, multidimensional nature of epistemic agency enables us to move beyond claims of “more” versus “less” epistemic agency and toward nuanced conversations about the aspects of agency that are necessary and appropriate to accomplish particular learning goals in and across moments, and what tensions and trade‐offs arise in doing so.
