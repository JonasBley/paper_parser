## 1. From wavefunctions to orbitals: a Mathematics–Physics–Chemistry model for quantum instruction
**Source:** European Journal of Physics | **Date:** 2026-09-17 | **Relevance Score:** 0.599
**Tags:** Educational Focus, Cognitive Frameworks, Multimedia & Representations, Quantum/Modern Curriculum

**LLM Reasoning:** The abstract is an empirical quantum education study proposing an interdisciplinary instructional model and measuring student conceptual understanding, so it is educational and not a review.

**Authors:** Truong Van Tuan, Tran Trong Tai

**Abstract:** Abstract Quantum physics is a foundational topic in the education of natural sciences, science teacher education, and engineering, but it is also one of the subjects that causes substantial conceptual difficulties for students. An important reason is that learners must simultaneously use three different knowledge languages: the mathematical language of wavefunctions and operators, the physical language of states and measurement, and the chemical language of orbitals, electronic structure, and spectra. When these three disciplinary languages and the representations used within them are taught separately, students may manipulate formulas without understanding their physical meaning, or they may describe orbitals in chemistry while still identifying them with the classical trajectory of an electron. This study proposes and evaluates an interdisciplinary Mathematics–Physics–Chemistry instructional model for undergraduate quantum physics, focusing on three core concepts: the wavefunction, quantum probability, and the orbital. The model is grounded in theories of representational translation, conceptual change, and interdisciplinary STEM education. The research design uses a mixed-methods quasi-experimental approach, with 150 students divided into an experimental group and a control group, together with 10 instructors who participated in the validation of the instruments and the instructional model. The results show that the experimental group achieved greater improvement in conceptual understanding and reported higher perceived interdisciplinary representational translation competence, including perceived ability to connect Mathematics, Physics, and Chemistry. Because intact classes were used, these findings are interpreted as intervention-associated differences rather than as fully isolated causal effects. Misconception-linked responses concerning ‘the orbital is a trajectory’, ‘ | ψ | 2 is the real shape of the electron’, and ‘wavefunction normalization is only an algebraic procedure’ decreased more markedly in the experimental group than in the control group after the intervention. The paper contributes an instructional framework that can be used in the education of science teacher candidates, natural science students, and engineering students, while also suggesting a direction for empirical research on quantum education through an interdisciplinary approach.

## 2. Toward thinking quantumly: Student reasoning about the enigma of QM
**Source:** APS PRPER | **Date:** 2026-09-09 | **Relevance Score:** 0.579
**Tags:** Educational Focus, Cognitive Frameworks

**LLM Reasoning:** The paper is an empirical physics education study of student reasoning and epistemological frames in quantum mechanics learning.

**Authors:** Marc L. Whiting, Lauren Barth-Cohen, Adrian L. Adams, Jordan Gerton, David Stroupe

**Abstract:** The physics education research (PER) literature on quantum mechanics (QM) often acknowledges and minimizes what we refer to as the enigma of QM, viewing these aspects of QM as a challenge to overcome, rather than an inherent feature to be leveraged for learning. Using the overlapping theories of knowledge-in-pieces and resource theory as connected to an epistemological framing perspective, we focus on students’ descriptions of the enigma of QM and the epistemological frames that students view as relevant when approaching QM. Data comes from interviews with 10 undergraduate physics students who had learned quantum physics topics across different courses in the curriculum, ranging from sophomore-level modern physics to an advanced undergraduate-level QM course. To elicit students’ ideas on QM, the interview protocol emphasized their perspectives and empowerment when possible. Iterative rounds of qualitative analysis coded the data for how students described enigmatic aspects of QM, along with three epistemological frames students identified as their reasoning strategies: reasoning about scale, reasoning about the connections between QM and mathematics, and reasoning about the connections between QM and classical mechanics. The results document that students are aware that something about QM is unexplainable, an enigma to acknowledge. We argue that acknowledgment of the enigma of QM is a key part of what we refer to as thinking quantumly. Additionally, the epistemological frame of reasoning about scale has not been widely discussed in the QM-PER literature, which students use to distinguish QM from classical mechanics and to explain why mathematical models are needed. These results are important for amplifying students’ voices in how they approach enigmatic aspects of QM. Thus, our results begin to move the QM-PER literature beyond viewing enigmatic aspects of QM as obstacles to be corrected through refined instruction, toward an asset-based orientation as a source of meaningful insights for the learning process.

## 3. Exploring preservice teachers’ use of ontologies and stances toward the wave function through a classical-quantum analogy lesson
**Source:** APS PRPER | **Date:** 2026-09-09 | **Relevance Score:** 0.569
**Tags:** Educational Focus, Quantum/Modern Curriculum

**LLM Reasoning:** The study is an empirical physics education investigation of preservice teachers' ontological reasoning and quantum mechanics teaching reform, not a review or technical quantum advance.

**Authors:** Paul de Haas, Hanna Stammes, Henk W. de Regt, Erik Barendsen

**Abstract:** Physics students, teachers, and preservice teachers (PSTs) struggle with the abstract nature of quantum mechanics (QM), which has led to calls for a more conceptual approach to QM teaching and learning. In response, this study uses a novel analogy between Kundt’s tube and the infinite quantum well to elicit discussion of ontological issues. We investigated PSTs’ use of ontologies, including their interpretation of the wave function. Nine PSTs participated in the analogy lesson; dialogues were recorded, transcribed, and thematically analyzed. We identified five different ontologies: one classical and four quantum ontologies. The importance of probabilities in PSTs’ quantum ontologies stood out. Three ontologies (probabilistic particle, particle-wave, and probabilistic wave) expand the particle-wave duality, while the fourth (wave collapse) added a dynamic element to this axis. PSTs often used a single ontology in response to one aspect of the analogy but switched flexibly between ontologies across aspects of the analogy that they discussed. Concerning the wave function, we found both realist and formalist stances, revealing a diversity of interpretations that contrasts with the uniformity of the formalist QM courses PSTs had attended. We conclude that explicit attention to ontological issues in QM, supported by classical-quantum analogies, provides insight into PSTs’ ontological reasoning and offers a conceptual approach to teaching and learning QM. We present recommendations for PST training and implications for QM teaching reform.

**LLM Reasoning:** The abstract explicitly frames the work as a pedagogical geometric visualization for teaching quantum and relativistic concepts to advanced students, so it is educational rather than a review or technical advance.

**Authors:** Natarajan TS

**Abstract:** Abstract The standard formalisms of quantum mechanics and special relativity are extraordinarily successful, yet several foundational features are introduced to students as postulates or mathematical constructs without physical visualisation: why the wavefunction must be complex, what is physically associated with spin, why time dilation occurs in material particles, where the Lorentz-factor scaling of inertia comes from, why energy quantization follows from a phase-closure condition, and what geometric account can be given of wave-particle duality. The model is fully consistent with standard quantum and relativistic predictions. Its contribution is pedagogical and interpretational: it offers a unified geometric visualisation that may help students develop intuition for these foundational concepts, rather than a derivation of quantum mechanics or special relativity from first principles. The paper is self-contained and addressed to advanced undergraduates and graduate students.
